# Money, Productivity, and Human Evolution

## The Core Metaphor: Money as Points

Humanity continues advancing and becoming more capable. There exists an inherent human desire to perceive this advancement. Money operates similarly to points in a game — printing additional money creates the feeling of progress. Possessing more points feels superior, even when fewer points with greater individual value would be economically identical.

In a functional system, increased productivity yields more points. These points accurately represent created value. However, points introduce a complication: individuals can manipulate them without genuine productivity. This separation between points and actual productivity represents a core system failure.

## The Distortion: When Points Become the Goal

Money functioned originally as a productivity indicator but has transformed into a competing objective. This exemplifies Goodhart's Law: "once a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

When people pursue point accumulation rather than the underlying productive activity, systems deteriorate. Financial engineering, speculation, and rent-seeking supplant actual creation and problem-solving.

## The Stress of a Growing Points System

Expanding points through printing worsens this issue. Additional money circulating means:

- Greater cognitive resources devoted to tracking and safeguarding points
- Diverted attention from building and creating
- An invisible focus tax affecting everyone
- Increased difficulty because effort goes toward point management alongside genuine development

When points determine survival, financial anxiety compounds the already demanding work of genuine human development.

## The Case for Rigidity

### Frugality and Presence

Under rigid monetary systems — where units maintain or appreciate in value — each transaction becomes deliberate. Spending demands presence and genuine evaluation of whether something justifies exchanging your stored effort.

This contrasts sharply with inflationary thinking encouraging immediate spending. Rigid systems cultivate respect for wealth transfer, enhancing how people navigate reality, make decisions, and relate to their labor.

### A Changed Relationship with Time

Inflation punishes patience while rewarding urgency, debt, and leverage. Rigid systems enable longer-term thinking without feeling that inaction means falling backward. This redirects energy toward slower, deeper, more meaningful work — the genuine productivity driving real progress.

### Honest Feedback

In rigid systems, wealth growth more clearly indicates actual value creation rather than asset inflation or financial manipulation. Signal clarity improves. People distinguish real contribution from point gaming more easily. The system becomes more transparent.

## Addressing the Counterarguments

### "Rigidity Causes Hoarding"

Inflation proponents claim it prevents hoarding by discouraging cash accumulation. However, hoarding persists under inflation — merely changing form. People hoard assets, property, and equity rather than currency.

Hoarding impulses stem from money representing stored value. Economic cooperation continues under inflation, but frequently serves accumulating more — hoarding under different names. Inflation doesn't eliminate hoarding; it obscures it.

### "Rigidity Kills Creativity and Risk-Taking"

Rigid systems amplify failure consequences. Every unit matters more, with no expanding pool buffering mistakes. This might theoretically increase fear and decrease experimentation.

However, this misses a deeper question: what genuinely drives healthy risk-taking?

Two authentic sources exist:

1. **Confidence** — understanding the domain sufficiently to believe success is probable. Risk becomes calculated, success rates higher, resources allocated efficiently. This benefits society.

2. **Passion** — loving the work itself, making the activity itself rewarding regardless of outcome. The practitioner remains whole even if commercial failure occurs. This generates authentic innovation.

Both establish healthy risk-reward balance. Confidence increases success probability. Passion rewards without requiring results. Rigid systems amplify both signals — resources flow toward people genuinely believing in their work or driven by love for it.

Without confidence or passion, systems compensate by printing money — artificially lowering risk costs so people attempt ventures without conviction. These attempts lack substance: success money becomes the motivation rather than the work itself. The outcome is volume without quality, activity resembling progress but lacking authenticity.

Betting markets illustrate this: more confident or passionate individuals bet more. The betting system itself signals conviction. Rigid monetary systems improve investment signal quality. Loose systems collapse signal-to-noise ratios — genuine conviction becomes indistinguishable from cheap speculation.

**Commercial innovation builds upon revolutionary creativity.** Initial breakthrough precedes commercial development. When profitability becomes clear, companies invest regardless of monetary rigidity. Money follows conviction.

### The Resource Curse as Evidence

Countries with abundant natural resources frequently become complacent and spend recklessly, facing reduced innovation pressure. Easy abundance breeds laziness. Easy money maps directly to this pattern: freely flowing capital without discipline produces activity resembling progress but lacking substance. Rigidity conversely might encourage higher-quality innovation — creativity emerging from overcoming genuine constraints.

## The Balance

Money serves as crucial social coordination. It enables millions of strangers cooperating impossibly otherwise. Some treat money instrumentally as fuel rather than destination. Structurally, systems reward treating it as destination, and inflation amplifies that tendency.

Inflation functions as friction — forcing engagement with time, risk, and impermanence. Societies abundant with confidence and passion might absorb monetary expansion without distortion, since people taking risks would do so for appropriate reasons regardless. Problems emerge when confidence and passion erode, and easy money becomes a replacement — prosthetic motivation substituting genuine conviction. Causality potentially runs both directions: easy money can erode conviction, but cultures losing conviction reach for easy money as support.

The deeper solution extends beyond monetary policy. It demands restoring conditions producing confidence and passion — education cultivating genuine curiosity, structures enabling real problem encounters, values honoring work over reward. Currently, balance tilts excessively toward points, and monetary systems amplify this tilt rather than correct it.

The actual question isn't whether points systems should exist, but whether we can maintain them without colonizing people's sense of direction.

## The Deeper Thesis

"Humans grow through meaningful resistance, not through the removal of friction."

Loose monetary policy removes friction appearing as progress but actually softening conditions producing genuine development. Systems with genuine constraints and real expansion costs restore resistance cultivating:

- Deliberate decision-making
- Long-term thinking
- Honest value signals
- Innovation from genuine passion and necessity
- Healthier human relationships with tools serving them

An even more resilient motivation than confidence or passion might exist: duty — acting because it's correct now, requiring no reward, intrinsic or otherwise. Confidence depends on expected success. Passion depends on doing experience. Duty depends on neither. Perhaps duty and passion represent identical motivations differently perceived — someone acting from duty actually feels passionate, but passion emerges from alignment with what requires doing rather than personal desire. Regardless, monetary systems crowding out all three — confidence, passion, and duty — favoring point-chasing have failed fundamentally.

## On the Current Moment

Today's world makes the rigidity case compelling. Reckless money printing created distortions rippling through economies and individual existence. Returning to gold-backed currency appeals because it represents an anchor — a constraint forcing honesty and discipline back into systems.

Gold itself has limitations — somewhat arbitrary supply, geographic concentration, and tying global economies to physical commodities introduce rigidities not always beneficial. However, the underlying principle endures: "we need a monetary system with genuine constraints and real costs to expansion." Specific mechanisms matter less than principles.

## Summary

Initial revolutionary creativity originates from humans — from confidence, passion, and duty. Capital serves as a tool extending it forward. When tools overshadow humans, when point management replaces genuine growth pursuit, something essential disappears. Solutions require more than monetary reform — restoring cultural and institutional conditions cultivating genuine conviction. Monetary systems matter because they either amplify or weaken those conditions. Progress involves restoring money to its proper place — as means, not end — and constructing frameworks maintaining it there.
